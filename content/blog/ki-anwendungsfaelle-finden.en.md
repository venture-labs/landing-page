    text: "Finding AI use cases is less a matter of creativity than of sequence. A three-step approach has proven useful: first collect what eats time in daily work, then rate each idea by benefit, effort, data situation and risk, and finally start with the smallest case that still makes a difference. That turns a wish list into an order a company can justify."

  - type: "paragraph"

Or there is a long list of ideas without a yardstick, and the case that wins is the one presented most loudly. Both rarely lead to a use case that is actually used day to day. Why AI projects in mid-sized companies fail at such points is described elsewhere."

  - type: "heading"
    text: "Step 1: collect what creates work today"
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "The start is not AI ideas but work steps. What is collected is whatever regularly costs time, causes errors or gets left undone. Typical candidates are sorting emails, assigning receipts, preparing quotes or pre-qualifying service requests. It is important to ask the people who do the work: where is something copied, retyped, looked up, asked about? Where is someone waiting for information that already exists somewhere?"
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "Each entry gets one sentence in plain form: who does what, how often, with which documents, and how one can tell it is done. If the last point cannot be answered, the idea is not yet a use case."
  - type: "heading"
    text: "Step 2: rate by benefit, effort, data and risk"
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "Each collected case is classified by four questions. A rough rating of high, medium and low is enough; false precision does not help."
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "Benefit: what changes if the step disappears or gets faster? Does it count in time, in errors, in customers' waiting time? Who would notice, and who would miss it if it went away again?"
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "Effort: how many systems are involved? Are inputs and outputs clear? A well-bounded process with few interfaces is much easier than one that runs through many departments."
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "Data situation: is the information that is needed available digitally and reasonably organised? An assistant that must answer from scattered, outdated or non-digital documents can only be as good as those documents. This often decides whether a case is possible now or only after some tidying up."
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "Risk: what happens if the agent is wrong? For an internal pre-sort an error is annoying; for an email to a customer or for money it can be expensive. Where a mistake has consequences, a person belongs in the flow as approver, and the case becomes more elaborate."
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "The rating usually reveals a pattern: few cases have high benefit with low effort, usable data and small risk. Those are the candidates. Cases with high benefit but poor data go on a “prepare first” list. Cases with high risk and unclear benefit can be dropped deliberately."
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "It helps not to do the rating in management alone. People who do the work every day know the exceptions that appear in no process diagram, and they notice early whether a proposal would hold up in practice. Those who took part in the rating are also more likely to support the later rollout."
  - type: "heading"
    text: "Step 3: start with the smallest case"
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "From the candidates, the smallest case that still makes a visible difference is chosen, not the largest. A small case quickly delivers experience: how good are the results really? How does the team react? What data is missing? A project meant to change an entire department takes months before anyone sees anything, and often loses support in the meantime."
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "A small case comes with a goal whose success can be measured without flattering it, and a person in the company who is responsible for running it. It also includes deciding what the agent only prepares and what a person approves."
  - type: "heading"
    text: "An example of the rating"
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "An example, purely for illustration: a company collects three ideas. First, pre-sorting incoming emails by project; second, an assistant for questions from the manuals; third, automatic replies to customers. Pre-sorting has manageable effort, the data sits in the mailbox, and an error can be corrected. The manual assistant depends on whether the documents are in order. The automatic customer reply carries the highest risk and would get, if at all, approval by a person. The company would start with pre-sorting. The example is invented and shows only the logic, no measured values."  # prüfen: Invented illustration without figures; Christian decides whether it stays or is replaced by an example from our own operation
  - type: "heading"
    text: "Limits of this method"
  - type: "paragraph"
    text: "A rating by eye is not a calculation. It does not replace measuring before and after deployment, and it can miss cases that only appear when the team is asked. Not every process needs AI either. Sometimes a simple rule or a better workflow is enough, and that is a good result. Where AI does not pay off, that should be said honestly."
